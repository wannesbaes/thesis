\chapter{Rayleigh Fading Channel Model}
\label{appendix:rayleigh_channel}

This appendix derives the Rayleigh channel model, which is commonly used to characterize wireless propagation in environments with many scatterers.
Understanding the physical origin of this model provides useful intuition, since it serves as a fundamental building block for the more sophisticated channel models encountered throughout the dissertation.
The derivation proceeds in three steps.
First, the Doppler shift experienced by a moving \ac{RX} receiving a signal reflected by a single scatterer is derived in Section~\ref{appendix_section:doppler_shift}.
Second, this result is extended to a large number of independent scatterers in Section~\ref{appendix_section:cs_complex_gaussian_channel_gain}, and it is shown that the resulting channel gain converges to a circularly symmetric complex Gaussian random variable as the number of scatterers grows large.
Finally, we explore the time-varying nature of the channel in Section~\ref{appendix_section:doppler_spectrum_jakes_model}, and derive the autocorrelation function and power spectral density of the fading process.

\section{Doppler Shift}
\label{appendix_section:doppler_shift}
% =====================
%    Doppler Shift 
% =====================

Consider a \ac{TX} transmitting a baseband signal $x(t)$, modulated onto a carrier of frequency $f_c$.
The signal reaches the \ac{RX} after being reflected by a single scatterer, introducing an attenuation $a_\alpha$.
For now, the \ac{RX} is assumed to be stationary, so the path length $d_0$ and hence the propagation delay $\tau_0 = d_0/c$ are constant.
The received passband signal is given by
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{PB}}(t)
    = \Re\!\left\{ a_\alpha\, x(t - \tau_0)\, e^{j2\pi f_c (t - \tau_0)} \right\}.
\end{equation}

Now suppose the \ac{RX} moves with velocity $v$, and let $\alpha$ denote the angle between the direction of motion and the direction of the incoming wave.
The component of velocity along the propagation path is $v\cos(\alpha)$, so the path length, and hence the propagation delay, become time-varying.
This scenario is illustrated in~\autoref{fig:doppler_shift}.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/annex/C_rayleigh/doppler_shift.png}
    \caption[Doppler shift]
    {An illustration of the scenario where the receiver experiences a Doppler shift due to its motion relative to the incoming signal reflected by a scatterer.}
    \label{fig:doppler_shift}
\end{figure}

The time-varying propagation delay is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:time_varying_delay}
    \tau(t)
    = \frac{d_0 - v\cos(\alpha)\,t}{c}
    = \tau_0 - \frac{v\cos(\alpha)}{c}\,t.
\end{equation}
Substituting~\eqref{eq:time_varying_delay} into the expression for the received
passband signal and expanding the phase term gives
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{PB}}(t)
    = \Re\!\left\{
        a_\alpha \,
        x\!\left((1 + \tfrac{v\cos(\alpha)}{c}) t - \tau_0\right) \,
        e^{j2\pi (f_c + f_D\cos(\alpha))\, t} \,
        e^{-j2\pi f_c \tau_0}
      \right\},
\end{equation}
where $f_D = v / \lambda$ is the maximum Doppler frequency and $\lambda = c / f_c$ is the carrier wavelength.

Two simplifying assumptions are now introduced to obtain a tractable baseband model.
The first is the narrowband assumption: the bandwidth $B$ of $x(t)$ is assumed to be much smaller than the carrier frequency, $B \ll f_c$.
Since $x(t)$ varies slowly compared to the carrier, the small time dilation $v\cos(\alpha)/c \ll 1$ causes negligible distortion of the baseband envelope.
The second assumption is perfect synchronization at the \ac{RX}, so that the initial delay $\tau_0$ can be neglected as well.
Together, these two approximations give
\begin{equation}
    x\!\left(\!\left(1 + \tfrac{v\cos(\alpha)}{c}\right) t - \tau_0\right)
    \approx
    x(t).
\end{equation}

Applying both approximations and downconverting to baseband yields the received signal
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:received_signal_single_scatterer}
    y(t) = h(t) \; x(t),
    \qquad \text{with} \quad
    h(t) = a_\alpha\, e^{\,j2\pi f_D\cos(\alpha)\,t\; +\; j\phi_\alpha},
\end{equation}
where $\phi_\alpha = -2\pi f_c \tau_0$ is the initial carrier phase accumulated over
the path length $d_0$.

The channel $h(t)$ is a complex exponential with constant amplitude $|h(t)| = a_\alpha$ and a phase that advances linearly at the Doppler frequency $f_D \cos(\alpha)$.
In practice, even sub-wavelength changes in $d_0$ cause variations of order $2\pi$ in $\phi_\alpha$, so it is customary to model $\phi_\alpha$ as uniformly distributed over $[0, 2\pi)$ and independent across different scatterers.

\section{CS Complex Gaussian Channel Gain}
\label{appendix_section:cs_complex_gaussian_channel_gain}
% =========================================
%    CS Complex Gaussian Channel Gain
% =========================================

Consider the general scenario in which the signal from the \ac{TX} reaches the \ac{RX} via $N$ independent scatterers.
The scatterers are assumed to be uniformly distributed on a sphere around the \ac{RX}, so that the angles of arrival $\alpha_n$ are independent and identically distributed.
Each scatterer $n$ is further characterized by an initial phase $\phi_n$, which is uniformly distributed over $[0, 2\pi)$ and independent across scatterers, as established in Section~\ref{appendix_section:doppler_shift}.
The individual attenuations are set to $a_n = 1/\sqrt{N}$, so that the total expected received power remains normalized to unity for any value of $N$.
By superposition of the single-scatterer result in~\eqref{eq:received_signal_single_scatterer}, the aggregate channel gain reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:aggregate_channel_gain}
    h(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n \, e^{\,j(2\pi f_D \cos(\alpha_n)\, t \;+\; \phi_n)}.
\end{equation}

To characterize the distribution of $h(t)$, the real and imaginary parts are analyzed separately.
Let $\theta_n \triangleq 2\pi f_D \cos(\alpha_n)\, t$ denote the deterministic phase contribution of scatterer $n$ at time $t$, and define
\begin{equation}
    X(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n \cos(\theta_n + \phi_n),
    \qquad
    Y(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n \sin(\theta_n + \phi_n),
\end{equation}
so that $h(t) = X(t) + j\,Y(t)$.
The mean, variance, and covariance of these components are computed below for a fixed time instant~$t$.

\paragraph*{Mean:}~\\
Since $\phi_n$ is uniformly distributed over $[0, 2\pi)$ and independent of $\alpha_n$, the expectation of each summand can be evaluated conditionally on $\alpha_n$ using the law of total expectation:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    \mathbb{E}\!\left[X(t)\right]
    &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}\!\left[a_n \cos(\theta_n + \phi_n)\right] \\
    &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[a_n \cos(\theta_n + \phi_n) \mid \alpha_n\right]\right] \\
    &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}\!\left[a_n \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos(\theta_n + \phi_n)\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}\phi_n\right] \\
    &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}\!\left[a_n \; 0\right]
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Therefore, $\mathbb{E}[X(t)] = 0$.
An identical argument with sine in place of cosine gives $\mathbb{E}[Y(t)] = 0$, and thus $\mathbb{E}[h(t)] = 0$.

\paragraph*{Variance:}~\\
Since $\mathbb{E}[X(t)] = 0$, the variance reduces to the second moment, which can be expanded as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Var}[X(t)]
    = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}
      \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[a_n \cos(\theta_n + \phi_n) \, a_m \cos(\theta_m + \phi_m) \mid \alpha_n, \alpha_m\right]\right],
\end{equation}
where the law of total expectation is applied again to evaluate the expectation of each summand conditionally on $\alpha_n$ and $\alpha_m$.
For $n \neq m$, the independence of $\phi_n$ and $\phi_m$ allows the expectation to factorize into a product of two zero-mean terms, so the cross terms vanish.
For $n = m$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\!\left[a_n^2 \cos^2(\theta_n + \phi_n) \mid \alpha_n\right]
    = a_n^2 \int_{0}^{2\pi} \cos^2(\theta_n + \phi_n)\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}\phi_n
    = \frac{1}{2} \, a_n^2.
\end{equation}
Summing the diagonal terms and substituting $a_n = 1/\sqrt{N}$ gives $\mathrm{Var}[X(t)] = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n^2 = 1/2$.
An analogous calculation gives $\mathrm{Var}[Y(t)] = 1/2$.

\paragraph*{Covariance:}~\\
Since $\mathbb{E}[X(t)] = \mathbb{E}[Y(t)] = 0$, the covariance reduces to the cross moment, which can be expanded as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Cov}[X(t),Y(t)]
    = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}
      \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[a_n \cos(\theta_n + \phi_n) \, a_m \sin(\theta_m + \phi_m) \mid \alpha_n, \alpha_m\right]\right].
\end{equation}
Similarly to the variance calculation, the cross terms vanish for $n \neq m$, and for $n = m$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\!\left[a_n^2 \cos(\theta_n + \phi_n)\sin(\theta_n + \phi_n) \mid \alpha_n\right]
    = \frac{1}{2} a_n^2 \int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin(2\theta_n + 2\phi_n)\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{d}\phi_n
    = 0.
\end{equation}
Hence, $\mathrm{Cov}[X(t),Y(t)] = 0$, confirming that the real and imaginary parts of the channel gain are uncorrelated.
\bigskip

Each summand in $X(t)$ is a bounded, zero-mean random variable, and the summands are mutually independent.
By the Central Limit Theorem~\cite{central_limit_theorem_1935}, as the number of scatterers grows large ($N \to \infty$), the distribution of $X(t)$ converges to a Gaussian with mean $0$ and variance $1/2$.
The same holds for $Y(t)$.
Since $X(t)$ and $Y(t)$ are independent Gaussian random variables, they are jointly Gaussian, and the channel gain $h(t) = X(t) + j\,Y(t)$ is therefore a circularly symmetric complex Gaussian random variable with zero mean and unit variance:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:channel_gain_distribution}
    h(t) \sim \mathcal{CN}(0,\,1).
\end{equation}
A standard transformation to polar coordinates shows that the amplitude $|h(t)|$ follows a Rayleigh distribution, which is the origin of the model's name.
The phase $\angle h(t)$ is uniformly distributed over $[0, 2\pi)$, as a direct consequence of the circular symmetry of $\mathcal{CN}(0,1)$.
Finally, the instantaneous power $|h(t)|^2$ follows an exponential distribution with unit mean. 

\section{Doppler Spectrum}
\label{appendix_section:doppler_spectrum}
% =======================
%    Doppler Spectrum 
% =======================

Section~\ref{appendix_section:cs_complex_gaussian_channel_gain} characterized the distribution of $h(t)$ at a fixed time instant $t$.
To describe the temporal variation of the fading process, the \acf{acf} and the \acf{PSD} of $h(t)$ are derived in this section.

As established in Section~\ref{appendix_section:cs_complex_gaussian_channel_gain}, the channel gain at time $t$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    h(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n \, e^{\,j(2\pi f_D \cos(\alpha_n)\, t \;+\; \phi_n)},
\end{equation}
where the expected power is normalized, i.e., $\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}[a_n^2] = 1$ and $\phi_n \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, 2\pi)$ are independent across scatterers.
The scatterers are further assumed to be uniformly distributed on a sphere around the \ac{RX}, so that the angles of arrival are identically distributed as $\alpha_n \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, 2\pi)$.

\subsection*{Autocorrelation Function}
\label{appendix_section:acf_jakes_model}

Let us calculate the \ac{acf} $R_h(\tau) = \mathbb{E}[h(t) h^*(t-\tau)]$ of the fading process:
\begin{fleqn}[0em]
\label{eq:acf_calculation}
\begin{align*}
    &R_h(\tau) \\
    &\quad= \mathbb{E}\Big[ \big( \sum_{n=0}^{N} a_n \, \exp(j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_n) \, t + j \phi_n) \big) \, \big( \sum_{m=0}^{N} a_m \, \exp(-j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_m) \, (t-\tau) - j \phi_m) \big) \Big] \\
    &\quad= \sum_{n=0}^{N} \sum_{m=0}^{N} \mathbb{E}\big[ a_n \,\exp(j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_n) \, t + j \phi_n) \; a_m \, \exp(-j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_m) \, (t-\tau) - j \phi_m) \big] \\
    &\quad= \sum_{n=0}^{N} \sum_{m=0}^{N} \mathbb{E}\big[ a_n a_m \exp(j \, 2\pi \, f_D \left( \cos\alpha_n - \cos\alpha_m \right) t) \, \exp(j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_m) \, \tau) \, \exp(j \phi_n) \, \exp(-j \phi_m) \big] \\
    &\quad= \sum_{n=0}^{N} \sum_{m=0}^{N} \mathbb{E}\big[ a_n a_m \exp(j \, 2\pi \, f_D \left( \cos\alpha_n - \cos\alpha_m \right) t) \; \exp(j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_m) \, \tau) \big] \; \mathbb{E}\big[ \exp(j (\phi_n - \phi_m)) \big] \\
    & \qquad \bullet \; \mathbb{E}\big[ \exp(j (\phi_n - \phi_m)) \big] = 
    \begin{cases}
        \mathbb{E}\left[ e^{j (\phi_n - \phi_n)}\right] = 1 & \text{if } n = m \\
        \mathbb{E}\left[ e^{j \phi_n}\right] \, \mathbb{E}\left[ e^{-j \phi_m}\right] = 0 & \text{if } n \neq m
    \end{cases}\\
    &\quad= \sum_{n=0}^{N} \mathbb{E}\big[ a_n^2 \, e^{j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_n) \, \tau}\big] \\
    &\quad= \sum_{n=0}^{N} \mathbb{E}\Big[ \mathbb{E}\big[ a^2_n \, e^{j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_n) \, \tau} \big| \alpha_n \big] \Big] \\
    &\quad= \sum_{n=0}^{N} \mathbb{E}\Big[ a_n^2 \int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_n) \, \tau} \, \frac{1}{2\pi} \, d\alpha_n \Big] \\
    &\quad= \sum_{n=0}^{N} \, \mathbb{E}\big[ a_n^2 \big] \; \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha_n) \, \tau} \, d\alpha \\
    &\quad= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha) \, \tau} \, d\alpha \\
    &\quad= J_0(2\pi f_D \tau).
\end{align*}
\end{fleqn}

The \ac{acf} of the channel gain is therefore given by the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind, $J_0(2\pi f_D \tau)$, which quantifies the degree of correlation between the channel gain at two time instants separated by $\tau$.
The \ac{acf} equals one at $\tau = 0$, since the channel gain is perfectly correlated with itself at zero lag, and decays as $\tau$ increases.
The channel coherence time $T_c$ is defined as the lag at which the \ac{acf} drops to a prescribed threshold, here taken as $R_h(T_c) = 1/2$.
For $\tau \ll T_c$, consecutive channel samples are highly correlated and the channel remains approximately constant over the interval.
For $\tau \gg T_c$, the \ac{acf} approaches zero and the samples are approximately uncorrelated.

The relationship between $T_c$ and $f_D$ is obtained as follows:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relation_Tc_fD}
    R_h(T_c) = J_0(2\pi f_D T_c) = \frac{1}{2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad T_c \approx \frac{0.25}{f_D}.
\end{equation}
The coherence time is therefore inversely proportional to the maximum Doppler frequency.
This makes intuitive sense: the faster the \ac{RX} moves, and thus the higher the Doppler frequency $f_D = v/\lambda$, the more rapidly the channel fluctuates, and hence the shorter the coherence time.

When the symbol duration is much smaller than the coherence time ($T_s \ll T_c$), the channel gain is approximately constant over many symbols.
In this slow fading regime, it is common to adopt the block fading model, in which $h(t)$ is assumed to be constant over a block of $L$ symbols and drawn independently from $\mathcal{CN}(0,1)$ for each successive block.

\subsection*{Power Spectral Density}
\label{appendix_section:psd_jakes_model}

By the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, the \ac{PSD} of a wide-sense stationary process equals the Fourier transform of its \ac{acf}.
Let us compute the \ac{PSD} $S_h(f) = \mathcal{F}\{R_h(\tau)\}$ of the fading process:
\begin{fleqn}[0em]
\begin{align*}
    &S_h(f) \\
    &\quad= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} R_h(\tau) \; e^{-j 2 \pi f \tau} \, d\tau \\
    &\quad= \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \left( \frac{1}{2\pi} \; \int_{0}^{2\pi} e^{j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha) \, \tau} \, d\alpha \right) \; e^{-j 2 \pi f \tau} \, d\tau \\
    &\quad= \frac{1}{2\pi} \; \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{j \, 2\pi \, f_D \cos(\alpha) \, \tau} \; e^{-j 2 \pi f \tau} \, d\tau \right) \, d\alpha \\
    &\quad= \frac{1}{2\pi} \; \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-j \, 2\pi \, (f - f_D \cos(\alpha)) \, \tau} \, d\tau \right) \, d\alpha \\
    & \qquad \bullet \; \text{definition Dirac delta function: } \\
    & \qquad \qquad\delta(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-j \, 2\pi \, \xi \, t} \, dt \iff \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} e^{-j \, 2\pi \, (f - f_D \cos(\alpha)) \, \tau} \, d\tau = \delta(f - f_D \cos(\alpha)) \\
    &\quad= \frac{1}{2\pi} \; \int_{0}^{\pi} \delta(f - f_D \cos(\alpha)) \, d\alpha \; + \; \frac{1}{2\pi} \; \int_{\pi}^{2\pi} \delta(f - f_D \cos(\alpha)) \, d\alpha \\ 
    & \qquad \bullet \; \text{substitution: } \\
    & \qquad \qquad u = f_D \cos(\alpha) \\
    & \qquad \qquad \Leftrightarrow \; du = -f_D \sin(\alpha) \, d\alpha  \\
    & \qquad \qquad \Leftrightarrow \; du = -f_D \; \; \text{sgn}(\sin\alpha) \, \sqrt{1 - \cos^2(\alpha)} \; d\alpha \\
    & \qquad \qquad \Leftrightarrow \;du = -f_D \; \; \text{sgn}(\sin\alpha) \, \sqrt{1 - (\frac{u}{f_D})^2} \; d\alpha \\
    & \qquad \qquad \alpha = 0, \alpha = 2\pi \implies u = f_D; \quad \alpha = \pi \implies u = -f_D \\
    &\quad= \frac{1}{2\pi} \; \int_{f_D}^{-f_D} \frac{\delta(f - u)}{-f_D \sqrt{1 - (\frac{u}{f_D})^2}} \,du \; + \; \frac{1}{2\pi} \; \int_{-f_D}^{f_D} \frac{\delta(f - u)}{f_D \sqrt{1 - (\frac{u}{f_D})^2}} \, du \\
    &\quad= \frac{1}{\pi} \; \int_{-f_D}^{f_D} \frac{\delta(f - u)}{f_D \sqrt{1 - (\frac{u}{f_D})^2}} \, du \\
    &\quad= \frac{1}{\pi} \; \frac{1}{f_D \sqrt{1 - (\frac{u}{f_D})^2}} \Bigg|_{u=f} \;, \qquad \text{for} \quad |f| < f_D \\
    &\quad= \frac{1}{\pi f_D \, \sqrt{1 - (\frac{f}{f_D})^2}} \;, \qquad \text{for} \quad |f| < f_D
\end{align*}
\end{fleqn}

The resulting \ac{PSD} is known as the Doppler spectrum.
It is nonzero only for $|f| \leq f_D$, confirming that the fading process is band-limited to the maximum Doppler frequency: since no scattering angle can produce a Doppler shift exceeding $\pm f_D$. The total Doppler spread equals $2f_D$.

The spectrum exhibits a characteristic ``bathtub'' shape, which is a direct consequence of the mapping $f = f_D \cos(\alpha)$.
Near $\alpha = 0$ and $\alpha = \pi$, $\cos(\alpha)$ varies slowly as a function of $\alpha$, so a wide range of angles maps to a narrow frequency band near $\pm f_D$, concentrating power at the spectral edges.
Near $\alpha = \pi/2$, $\cos(\alpha)$ varies rapidly, so comparatively few angles contribute per unit frequency around $f = 0$.
