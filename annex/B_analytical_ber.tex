\chapter{Analytical BER of an AWGN Channel}
\label{appendix:analytical_ber}

In this appendix, an analytical expression for the \acf{BER} of an uncoded transmission over an \acf{AWGN} channel is derived.

The \ac{TX} maps $m_c$ \acf{i.i.d.} bits onto a data symbol $a$ drawn from the constellation $\mathcal{A}$, which contains $M = 2^{m_c}$ distinct symbols.
The constellation is assumed to be normalized, i.e. $\mathbb{E}[|a|^2] = 1$. Let $p$ denote the transmit power per symbol. The actual channel input is then given by $\sqrt{p}\,a$.
Transmission takes place over an \ac{AWGN} channel, so that the received signal can be written as
\begin{equation}
    y = \sqrt{p}\,a + n,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is a zero-mean \ac{CS} complex Gaussian noise sample with variance $N_0$.
For convenience, the receiver works with the normalized decision variable $z = y / \sqrt{p}$, which allows the detection problem to be formulated directly in terms of the normalized constellation $\mathcal{A}$ and an equivalent noise sample $w$ with variance $N_0/p$.

Based on the decision variable $z$ and the constellation $\mathcal{A}$, the detector at the \ac{RX} decides in favor of a symbol $\hat{a}$.
The optimal detector is the one that minimizes the probability of decision error.
For an \ac{AWGN} channel and under the assumption of \ac{i.i.d.} input bits, the optimal decision rule reduces to the minimum-distance decision rule, which selects the constellation symbol closest to $z$ in Euclidean distance.
Finally, the demapper maps the detected symbol $\hat{a}$ back to $m_c$ bits, denoted by $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$.
A block diagram of this transmission chain is shown in \autoref{fig:awgn_channel}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/annex/B_analytical_ber/out/awgn_channel.pdf}
    \caption{Block diagram of an \acs{AWGN} channel}
    \label{fig:awgn_channel}
\end{figure}

The mapper, detector, and demapper are deterministic functions, whereas the transmitted symbol $a$ and the noise sample $n$ are random variables.
Consequently, the decision variable $z$ and the detected symbol $\hat{a}$ are also random variables, both of which depend on the transmitted symbol and the noise realization.
Conditioned on the transmitted symbol $a$, the decision variable is \ac{CS} complex Gaussian with mean $a$ and variance $N_0/p$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditional_pdf_decision_variable}
    z \mid a \sim \mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\!\left(a, \frac{N_0}{p}\right).
\end{equation}

% paragraph about the BER definition
The \ac{BER} is defined as the probability that a transmitted bit is detected incorrectly.
Equivalently, it can be expressed as the average number of bit errors per detected symbol, normalized by the number of bits per symbol:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ber_definition}
    \mathrm{BER} = \frac{1}{\log_2(M)} \sum_{(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha) \in \mathcal{A}^{2}} \mathrm{N}(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha)\, \mathrm{Prob}[\hat{a} = \hat{\alpha} \land a = \alpha].
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathcal{A}^{2}$ denotes the Cartesian product of the constellation with itself, and thus contains all possible transmitted-detected symbol pairs.
The quantity $\mathrm{N}(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha)$ denotes the number of bit errors incurred when the symbol $\alpha$ is transmitted and $\hat{\alpha}$ is detected.
In other words, it equals the Hamming distance between the binary labels of $\alpha$ and $\hat{\alpha}$.
Finally, $\mathrm{Prob}[\hat{a} = \hat{\alpha} \land a = \alpha]$ is the joint probability that $\alpha$ is transmitted and $\hat{\alpha}$ is detected.

Since the transmission is uncoded and the input bits are \ac{i.i.d.}, all constellation symbols are equiprobable.
The \ac{BER} expression can therefore be simplified using Bayes' rule as
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:ber_simplified}
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{BER}
    &= \frac{1}{\log_2(M)} \sum_{(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha) \in \mathcal{A}^{2}} \mathrm{N}(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha)\, \mathrm{Prob}[\hat{a} = \hat{\alpha} \mid a = \alpha]\, \mathrm{Prob}[a = \alpha] \\
    &= \frac{1}{M \log_2(M)} \sum_{(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha) \in \mathcal{A}^{2}} \mathrm{N}(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha) \, \mathrm{Prob}[\hat{a} = \hat{\alpha} \mid a = \alpha].
\end{align}
\end{subequations}


\section{Union Upper Bound}
\label{appendix_section:union_upper_bound}
% =========================
%   Union Upper Bound
% =========================

Consider the pairwise error event in which the transmitted symbol $\alpha$ is detected as a different symbol $\hat{\alpha}$, with $\hat{\alpha} \neq \alpha$.
Under the minimum-distance decision rule, the detector decides in favor of $\hat{\alpha}$ if and only if the decision variable $z$ falls inside the decision region associated with $\hat{\alpha}$.
Hence,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Prob}[\hat{a} = \hat{\alpha} \mid a = \alpha] = \mathrm{Prob}[z \in \mathcal{D}(\hat{\alpha}) \mid a = \alpha],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}(\hat{\alpha})$ denotes the decision region of $\hat{\alpha}$, i.e. the set of all points in the complex plane that are closer to $\hat{\alpha}$ than to any other constellation symbol.

An exact evaluation of this conditional decision probability is generally cumbersome because the decision region may be bounded by several neighboring constellation points.
A more tractable expression is obtained by replacing the full decision event by a necessary condition.
Indeed, if $\hat{\alpha}$ is detected when $\alpha$ was transmitted, then the decision variable must at least be closer to $\hat{\alpha}$ than to $\alpha$.
This condition is necessary, but not sufficient, since yet another constellation point may still be closer to $z$ than $\hat{\alpha}$.
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:upper_bound_pairwise_error_probability}
    \mathrm{Prob}[\hat{a} = \hat{\alpha} \mid a = \alpha] \leq \mathrm{Prob}[|z - \hat{\alpha}| < |z - \alpha| \mid a = \alpha].
\end{equation}
Geometrically, this upper bound corresponds to the probability that the noise pushes the decision variable across the perpendicular bisector between $\alpha$ and $\hat{\alpha}$, rather than all the way into the decision region of $\hat{\alpha}$.

The right-hand side of \eqref{eq:upper_bound_pairwise_error_probability} can be simplified as
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:upper_bound_pairwise_error_probability_simplification}
\begin{align}
    &\mathrm{Prob}[|z - \hat{\alpha}| < |z - \alpha| \mid a = \alpha] \notag \\
    & \quad = \mathrm{Prob}\left[|(\alpha + w) - \hat{\alpha}|^2 < |(\alpha + w) - \alpha|^2 \right] \\
    & \quad = \mathrm{Prob}\left[|w - (\hat{\alpha} - \alpha)|^2 - |w|^2 < 0 \right] \\
    & \quad = \mathrm{Prob}\left[|\hat{\alpha} - \alpha|^2 - 2 \Re\{w \, (\hat{\alpha} - \alpha)^*\} < 0 \right] \\
    & \quad = \mathrm{Prob}\left[\Re\{w \, \frac{(\hat{\alpha} - \alpha)^*}{|\hat{\alpha} - \alpha|}\} > \frac{|\hat{\alpha} - \alpha|}{2} \right] \\
    & \quad = \mathrm{Q} \left( \sqrt{\frac{p}{2 N_0}} \, |\hat{\alpha} - \alpha| \right) \label{eq:simplified-upper-bound}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
In the last step, the conditional inequality is expressed as the tail probability of a real Gaussian random variable.
Indeed, the quantity inside the probability operator is the projection of the \ac{CS} complex Gaussian noise $w$ onto a unit-magnitude complex direction.
Such a multiplication only rotates $w$ in the complex plane and therefore leaves its distribution unchanged.
Consequently, the projected quantity is a zero-mean real Gaussian random variable with variance equal to half the variance of $w$, i.e. $N_0/(2p)$.

Substituting \eqref{eq:simplified-upper-bound} into \eqref{eq:ber_simplified} yields the following upper bound on the \ac{BER}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ber_union_upper_bound}
    \mathrm{BER} \leq \frac{1}{M \log_2(M)} \sum_{(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha) \in \mathcal{A}^{2}} \mathrm{N}(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha) \, \mathrm{Q} \left( \sqrt{\frac{p}{2 N_0}} \, |\hat{\alpha} - \alpha| \right).
\end{equation}
This expression is known as the union upper bound.

\section{Nearest Neighbour Approximation}
\label{appendix_section:nn_approximation}
% =======================================
%     Nearest Neighbour Approximation
% =======================================

The union upper bound in~\eqref{eq:ber_union_upper_bound} sums over all possible transmitted--detected symbol pairs.
For moderate to high \ac{SNR}, however, the Q-function decays rapidly with increasing argument, so the dominant contributions arise from pairs $(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha)$ with the smallest inter-symbol distance.
It is therefore common to retain only the nearest-neighbour terms in the sum, i.e. those located at the minimum Euclidean distance $d_{\min}$ between the constellation points in $\mathcal{A}$.
Letting $\mathcal{S}(\alpha)$ denote the set of nearest neighbours of symbol $\alpha$ in $\mathcal{A}$, the approximation reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ber_nn_approximation_with_N}
    \mathrm{BER} 
    \approx \frac{1}{M \log_2(M)} \, \mathrm{Q}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{2N_0}}\,d_{\min}\right) \, \sum_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \sum_{\hat{\alpha} \in \mathcal{S}(\alpha)} \mathrm{N}(\hat{\alpha}, \alpha).
\end{equation}

For uncoded transmission, the optimal mapping from bits to symbol is given by Gray coding, which ensures that the binary labels of any two nearest-neighbour symbols differ in only one bit position, i.e. their Hamming distance is equal to one.
Hence,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{BER} \approx \frac{1}{M \log_2(M)}\; \mathrm{Q}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{2N_0}}\,d_{\min}\right) \sum_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} |\mathcal{S}(\alpha)|.
\end{equation}
where $|\mathcal{S}(\alpha)|$ denotes the number of nearest neighbours of symbol $\alpha$.
The remaining sum counts the total number of nearest-neighbour pairs in the constellation.
Dividing by $M$ gives the average number of nearest neighbours per symbol,
\begin{equation}
    K = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} |\mathcal{S}(\alpha)|,
\end{equation}
which is a constant that depends only on the type and size of the constellation.
The values of $K$ and $d_{\min}$ for all common modulation constellations, normalized to unit average symbol energy, are listed in \autoref{table:K_and_dmin_values}.

The final nearest-neighbour \ac{BER} approximation reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ber_nn_approximation}
    \mathrm{BER} \approx \frac{K}{\log_2(M)}\; \mathrm{Q}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{2N_0}}\,d_{\min}\right).
\end{equation}
